# Chapter 2: Before and After Flight

## Overview and operating boundaries

In this lesson we look at what a good pilot does before the engine starts, while it runs on the ground and after the flight has ended. Understanding the reasons behind each step matters far more than memorising a sequence.

The central idea is continuity: a safe flight begins before the pilot reaches the aeroplane and ends only after the aircraft is secured, defects are communicated and the records are complete. Good preparation removes avoidable uncertainty. Good post-flight work prevents the next pilot from inheriting an undisclosed problem.

> **Training guidance, not an operating checklist.** Use the current aircraft-specific Pilot’s Operating Handbook (POH), approved checklists, instructor guidance and applicable local rules. Switch positions, fuel grades, starter limits, oil-pressure deadlines, power settings and emergency actions vary between aircraft. Do not use this lesson to operate an aircraft without qualified instruction.

By the end of the chapter, you should be able to explain how personal fitness, documentation and aircraft condition influence a go/no-go decision; describe a systematic external and internal inspection; explain what is monitored around engine start and shutdown; and identify the actions that close a flight properly.

## 2a · Personal preparation and fitness to fly

Personal preparation has two timescales. In advance, understand the purpose of the lesson, revise the relevant theory and prepare navigation material where appropriate. On the day, allow enough time to obtain a briefing, assess conditions and inspect the aircraft without pressure to rush. Being early is not merely convenient: it gives you time to resolve a discrepancy instead of ignoring it to meet a departure time.

Assess fitness before leaving home and reassess it at the aerodrome. Illness, fatigue, stress, alcohol and medication can impair judgement and coordination. A cold or blocked ears may also make pressure changes painful and interfere with hearing or concentration. Feeling capable of driving to the aerodrome does not establish fitness to fly.

- **Illness:** consider symptoms, discomfort and their effect on attention. Discuss uncertainty with your instructor or an appropriate medical professional.
- **Medication:** prescribed and non-prescribed products can have side effects. Do not assume that an ordinary cold remedy is acceptable for flight.
- **Rest and stress:** recognise poor sleep, distraction and a strong desire to complete a lesson as factors in your decision.
- **Alcohol:** comply with current legal and school restrictions. The historical example in the source is not a universal or current permission to fly after a particular elapsed time.
- **Equipment and clothing:** bring the documents, charts, headset and other equipment required for the flight. Choose practical clothing and footwear that do not restrict movement or interfere with the pedals.

**Example:** a student arrives on time but reports ear congestion and poor sleep. Finishing the booking is not the objective; safe learning is. Tell the instructor before the flight and agree whether to postpone, change the activity or seek advice. Do not conceal the symptoms to avoid disappointing somebody.

## 2a · Flight authorisation, weather and operational planning

The flying school’s authorisation process establishes who is flying, which aircraft is being used and what activity has been approved. Check the authorisation details and relevant local procedures rather than treating the sheet as a formality. Local limitations, aerodrome arrangements and instructor restrictions may affect an otherwise reasonable plan.

Weather matters even for a short local lesson. Review suitable current reports and forecasts with your instructor, including visibility, cloud, wind and expected changes during the planned flight. Consider the conditions on return as well as those at departure. A forecast that permits take-off does not guarantee a safe or useful lesson throughout the period.

A cross-country flight adds route, airspace, navigation, fuel and diversion considerations. Whether a formal flight plan is required depends on the operation and applicable rules; it should not be inferred simply from whether a flight is described as “training”. Similarly, aerodrome booking-out arrangements are not the same thing as filing a formal flight plan.

Booking out informs the relevant Air Traffic Service or local reporting system of the intended activity. Supply the information required by that service and establish how the flight will be closed afterwards. Do not assume that a radio call, a school booking and a flight-plan submission are interchangeable.

| Preparation item | Purpose | Question to resolve before departure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flight authorisation | Confirms the approved aircraft, pilot and activity | Is the intended lesson authorised and within any restrictions? |
| Weather assessment | Supports a safe and useful flight | Are conditions suitable for the whole activity, including return or diversion? |
| Route and airspace review | Prevents navigation and airspace surprises | What clearances, restrictions or alternative routes may be relevant? |
| Fuel planning | Supports the intended flight and required margins | Is the usable quantity sufficient under the applicable requirements? |
| Booking out or flight plan | Supports operational reporting where required | What must be submitted, and who must be notified on completion? |

## 2a · Weight, balance and airworthiness

An aeroplane must remain within its approved mass and centre-of-gravity limits. Total mass affects performance; the position of that mass affects stability and controllability. Two people in the seats do not, by themselves, prove that a trainer is correctly loaded. Fuel, baggage, equipment, individual occupant weights and the aircraft’s current empty-weight data all matter.

Use the current weight-and-balance information and the school’s approved process. Assess both loading and the effect of fuel consumption where relevant. Stay within the aircraft’s limits rather than relying on a familiar seating arrangement or an earlier flight with different occupants.

Airworthiness also requires more than an attractive-looking aircraft. Review the relevant certification or operating documents, maintenance status, weight-and-balance schedule and technical log according to the aircraft and jurisdiction. Establish whether recorded defects have been rectified or are permissible under the applicable approved arrangements. This lesson does not determine whether a particular defect may legally be deferred.

The records and the physical inspection answer different questions. A maintenance entry cannot prove that a tyre has not been damaged since it was written. Conversely, a clean walk-around does not establish that required maintenance is current. Use both sources of evidence.

> **Decision point:** if a defect or document discrepancy leaves airworthiness uncertain, do not accept the aircraft for flight. Ask the instructor or an appropriately licensed engineer. An unresolved doubt is a reason to pause, not an invitation to invent an explanation.

## 2a · Starting the external inspection

Begin observing the aircraft as you approach it. Note wind direction, the available space, surrounding people, surface condition and the likely taxi path. Look for leaks, unusual aircraft attitude, unsecured covers and obvious damage. Identify suitable emergency equipment and ensure that the starting area will not expose people, nearby aircraft or buildings to propeller blast.

Use a consistent aircraft-specific walk-around sequence so that every required item is checked. The pattern in the illustration is a teaching example, not the mandatory route for every aircraft. A high-wing trainer, a low-wing trainer and an aircraft with different fuel-drain locations may need different sequences.

Before handling controls or moving surfaces, establish the aircraft’s safe condition according to its checklist. Typical considerations include ignition state, restraint against movement, removal of control locks and any required flap position. Never assume that a propeller is harmless because a key has been removed or the ignition switch appears to be off: an ignition-system fault can leave it capable of firing.

An interruption is a common way to omit a check. If somebody distracts you, return to a clearly identified point in the checklist rather than guessing where you stopped. Inspect the actual item and confirm its condition; remembering that it was serviceable yesterday is not an inspection.

## 2a · Fuel quantity, sampling and contamination

Fuel inspection asks several separate questions: is the fuel of the approved type, is there enough usable fuel for the intended operation, and is it free of unacceptable contamination? A satisfactory answer to one question does not settle the others. Fuel gauges provide useful information, but the aircraft-specific method of physically verifying quantity is an important cross-check.

Inspect the filler caps and their security. Follow the POH and approved procedures for sampling every required drain point, including after refuelling or other occasions specified for the aircraft. Use the appropriate sampler and safe handling procedures. Do not apply a single drain location or sampling schedule to all trainers.

In a conventional aviation-fuel sample, water can settle beneath the fuel because it is denser. A distinct lower layer, droplets or suspended contamination require investigation. Sediment is another warning. Fuel appearance and colour must be interpreted against the approved fuel type; colour alone does not prove that a sample is safe, and a container of water can be mistaken for a clear fuel sample if the check is careless.

## 2a · A systematic walk-around

Work around the aircraft in the sequence specified for the type. At each position, inspect the local parts and the surrounding structure. Damage, leaks, loose fasteners, buckled skin or displaced rivets may indicate a problem that is not visible from a distance. Do not attempt an unapproved repair or force a component to move simply because the checklist mentions it.

| Lights, aerials, panels and lockers | Security and absence of damage or loose items | A loose component can detach or interfere with operation. |

Continue around both sides rather than assuming symmetry means identical condition. Check the windscreen for visibility and the luggage compartments for security. Finish by stepping back for a general overview. Covers, chocks and tie-downs must be removed or retained at the appropriate stage while the aircraft remains safely restrained; do not release all restraints before there is an effective means of preventing movement.

## 2a · Internal inspection and passenger briefing

The internal inspection connects the pilot to the aircraft. Adjust and lock the seat, check restraint security and confirm that controls can be reached without stretching. An insecure seat can slide when force is applied to a pedal or control column; a comfortable position is not enough unless the locking mechanism is engaged.

Follow the approved cockpit inspection sequence for fuel selection, ignition, electrical services and control checks. Check full, free and correct movement of the flight controls by the prescribed method. Confirm that baggage, loose equipment and passenger limbs cannot obstruct the controls. Engine controls must also be checked as required for the type; arbitrary manipulation can affect starting conditions.

Scan the instrument panel systematically. Look for appropriate indications, required settings and abnormalities. An unexpected tripped circuit breaker is a discrepancy to investigate, not a reason to reset it repeatedly. Ensure that the necessary communications equipment, checklists and safety equipment are available and secured.

Before engine start, brief passengers on restraints, doors or exits, relevant emergency arrangements and how to avoid interfering with the controls. Explain any communication expectations appropriate to the lesson. A briefing is most useful when it relates to the actual cockpit rather than to an abstract list of emergency equipment.

**Self-check:** could you explain why a loose bag, unlocked seat and blocked static port create three different hazards? The bag may obstruct controls, the seat may move unexpectedly and the blocked port may mislead the pilot through instrument errors.

## 2b · Preparing for and understanding engine start

Starting introduces a moving propeller, noise, exhaust, electrical load and the possibility of aircraft movement. Confirm that the area and aircraft configuration are suitable before engaging the starter. Avoid exposing people, loose gravel, open hangars or nearby aircraft to propeller blast, and investigate any fuel spill before proceeding.

Use the exact starting checklist for the installed engine and fuel system. A conventional carburetted piston engine, a fuel-injected engine and an electronically controlled engine may have materially different procedures. Hot and cold starts can also differ. The source’s example settings and priming quantities are not universal instructions.

The purpose of priming, where fitted and required, is to provide an appropriate starting fuel charge. Too little may make ignition difficult; too much may flood the engine and create a fire hazard. The starter turns the engine until it can run under its own power, drawing a substantial electrical current in the process. Observe the approved starter duty cycle and cooling intervals rather than repeatedly cranking an engine that will not start.

Before the start, visually confirm the propeller area is clear and make the required warning call. A warning is an additional precaution, not proof that nobody is nearby. Keep looking and be prepared to stop the sequence if a person approaches or a hazard develops.

When the engine starts, release the starter as prescribed and verify disengagement through the indications appropriate to the aircraft. A starter that remains engaged is not a normal condition to ignore while continuing the lesson.

## 2b · After-start monitoring and the engine check

The first moments after start are an active monitoring phase. Check oil pressure against the POH’s required indication and time limit, along with engine behaviour and other required system indications. Do not adopt a generic deadline where the aircraft specifies something different.

| Observation | What it helps establish | Response to an abnormality |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starter indication or behaviour | The starter has disengaged correctly | Follow the approved abnormal procedure; do not continue as though the indication is normal. |
| Oil pressure and engine instruments | Lubrication and engine conditions are developing as expected | Apply the POH limits and shutdown or abnormal actions as required. |
| Stable idle and engine sound | The engine is operating consistently at the specified setting | Investigate roughness, unusual noise or unexpected behaviour with the instructor. |
| Electrical indication | The electrical system is behaving as expected after the start | Interpret the installed instrument correctly; not all aircraft use the same indication convention. |
| Vacuum or other system indications | Relevant systems have the required operating conditions | Assess only systems fitted to the aircraft and follow their specified limits. |
| Radio setup | Correct channel and usable audio | Confirm the appropriate frequency and settings before relying on communications. |

Magneto checks, where applicable, help establish operation of each independent ignition system. The instructor will teach the approved timing, power setting and acceptable indications. A small change may be expected, but allowable drops and differences are aircraft-specific. Rough running or an unacceptable indication requires a decision, not an improvised explanation.

The pre-take-off engine check is distinct from simply seeing that the engine idles after start. Carry it out at the appropriate designated location and under the approved checklist. Consider aircraft restraint, surface condition and propeller blast. Completion of an engine check does not replace the rest of the pre-take-off vital actions.

## 2b · Shutdown and an unexpectedly live propeller

Shutdown is a controlled transition from an operating engine to a safely parked aircraft. Follow the POH sequence, including any required cooling period, electrical shutdown steps, fuel-system actions and ignition checks. Turbocharged engines may have particular cooling requirements; use the applicable instructions rather than a single cooling time for all engines.

For many conventional piston trainers, mixture idle cut-off stops the engine by removing its fuel supply. Ignition is then secured as directed. Other engine installations use different controls. Identify what actually stops the engine on your aircraft and what makes the electrical and ignition systems safe afterwards.

Some ignition checks are intended to detect a failure of the magneto grounding arrangement. A faulty grounding circuit can leave a magneto live even with the ignition switch at OFF. A brief dead-cut check exists for this purpose, but some manufacturers advise against it. Do not perform it unless it is part of the approved procedure and your instructor has taught it for that aircraft.

The practical lesson is that an apparently stopped and switched-off engine does not make propeller handling safe. Treat the propeller as potentially live and keep people clear. Do not use a switch label as evidence that a real aircraft cannot start unexpectedly.

## 2b · Abnormal starts, flooding and fire

An engine that will not start is a reason to reassess, not to keep repeating the same attempt indefinitely. Excess fuel can make the mixture unsuitable for ignition; repeated cranking can discharge the battery and overheat the starter. Hot-start difficulties, fuel-system problems and ignition faults can look similar to an inexperienced pilot. Follow the approved abnormal-start procedure and involve the instructor.

A flooded-engine procedure differs from a normal start and depends on the installation. There is no universal switch-by-switch drill for it. Confirm the exact procedure and starter limitations before attempting recovery.

A fire during start is an emergency. Some approved procedures use continued cranking initially to draw excess fuel into the engine, alongside specified fuel and mixture actions. That is not a general instruction to keep cranking every burning engine. The aircraft’s emergency checklist and the instructor’s type-specific training determine the response.

If the fire is not extinguished, the priorities include the prescribed shutdown and securing actions, evacuation to a safe location and obtaining emergency assistance. Use suitable extinguishing equipment only when trained and safe to do so. Do not attempt another start after a fire or serious abnormal event until the aircraft has been appropriately assessed and released.

> **Before the first practical start:** identify the actual aircraft’s normal-start, abnormal-start and engine-fire checklists with your instructor, including the point at which the attempt must be abandoned. Classroom explanation cannot replace that preparation.

## 2c · Securing the aeroplane after flight

After shutdown, the aircraft remains vulnerable to wind, unintended movement and environmental damage. Park and secure it according to the aircraft instructions and aerodrome procedures. Consider the parking surface, wind and whether brakes, chocks and tie-downs are required. A parking brake is not a universal substitute for external restraint.

Carry out a brief external inspection for damage, leaks or any change noticed during the flight. Fit required covers and control locks, secure loose cabin items and restraints, close or lock doors as required, and return keys under the school’s process. Record or communicate the fitted covers and locks through the established handover arrangements so that the next pilot knows what must be removed.

Where approved and appropriate, refuelling after the last flight may reduce the space in which moist air can accumulate and condense overnight. It is not a universal instruction to fill every aircraft: fuel grade, loading needs, thermal expansion and local fuelling procedures must be considered.

Think of securing as a final inspection rather than a hurried departure from the aircraft. Ask: is it protected from movement, are the required covers and locks fitted, are there any unresolved defects, and does the next person have the information needed to accept it safely?

## 2c · Records, defect reporting and debrief

Close the operational reporting loop by booking in or closing the flight plan where required. Confirm the correct process; assume neither that a landing automatically closes every plan nor that somebody else will make the report. Accurate flight completion information supports the systems that monitor overdue aircraft.

Complete the authorisation sheet and aircraft records with the required times and details, using the school’s defined time conventions. Record defects factually: what was observed, when it occurred and under what conditions. “Radio audio became intermittent during taxi” is more useful than an unsupported diagnosis that the radio is broken. Do not erase or downplay an intermittent fault simply because it disappeared before shutdown.

Notify the instructor or engineer and make the required technical-log entry so that a defect cannot be lost between verbal handovers. The next pilot remains responsible for their own pre-flight assessment, but should not have to discover a known problem again. Where serviceability is uncertain, use the school’s procedure to prevent further operation pending assessment.

Complete your personal logbook under the applicable requirements. The training debrief then connects the flight to learning: discuss what went well, where workload increased, any procedural omissions and the preparation for the next lesson. An honest debrief is more valuable than describing a flight as faultless when a check was missed.

### Review scenarios

1. **A gauge and physical fuel check disagree.** Resolve the discrepancy using the approved method before accepting the quantity for planning. Neither indication should be dismissed without investigation.
2. **A loose fastener appears during the walk-around.** Stop and refer it to the instructor or engineer; do not assume it is cosmetic or perform an unapproved repair.
3. **An after-start indication remains abnormal.** Apply the aircraft’s limits and abnormal procedure instead of continuing in the hope that it will correct itself.
4. **An intermittent fault disappears before parking.** Report the observation and complete the required record. A disappearing symptom is not proof of rectification.
5. **The aircraft is parked but the reporting and records are unfinished.** The flight’s responsibilities are not complete. Secure the aircraft, close the reporting loop and finish the handover.

**Chapter takeaway:** preparation, engine procedures and post-flight duties form one chain of responsibility. Use a consistent checklist, understand the reason behind each check, respect aircraft-specific limitations and make uncertainty visible before it becomes somebody else’s hazard.
